# Example responses

Calibration examples for tone, depth and format. They are not templates to copy word for word. Always adapt to the user's situation and write in the user's language.

---

## Example 1. Short reply: "What do I say right now?" (mode C, Russian)

**User:**
> Поставщик на звонке говорит: «Это наша финальная цена, либо берёте, либо нет». Что ответить?

**Response:**

Это приём «бери или уходи» (take it or leave it). Не спорьте с ультиматумом и не принимайте его: спокойно верните разговор к обоснованию цены.

1. «Понимаю, что для вас это важный рубеж. Помогите мне понять, из чего складывается эта цифра, мне нужно будет обосновать её внутри компании».
2. «Давайте сверимся с рынком: у нас есть предложения в диапазоне X–Y. Что в вашем предложении оправдывает разницу?»
3. Если давят на срочность: «Решение такого размера я не принимаю на звонке. Вернусь к вам до четверга с ответом».

**Почему:** вопрос «как вы к этому пришли?» переводит торг с силы воли на объективные критерии, а пауза (микроальтернатива: «вернусь с ответом») защищает вас от решения под давлением.

**Ловушка:** не называйте свою предельную цену в ответ на ультиматум, это превратит разговор в торг позициями.

Если расскажете, сколько стоит альтернатива у других поставщиков и насколько вам важен именно этот, разберу всю ситуацию подробнее.

## Example 2. Short reply: concept question (English)

**User:**
> What's the difference between a BATNA and a bottom line?

**Response:**

A **bottom line** is a number you fix in advance: "I won't sell below $200k." It protects you from caving under pressure, but it's often arbitrary and stops you from noticing a creative deal that's better than it looks.

A **BATNA** (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement) is what you will actually do if there's no deal: sell to another buyer, rent the place out, wait a year. You compare every offer with that real alternative, not with a number you picked.

Practical rule: work out your BATNA first, then set a **trip wire** slightly above it. An offer below the trip wire doesn't mean an automatic "no"; it means "stop and think before agreeing."

## Example 3. Full analysis (mode A, English)

**User:**
> My manager told me there's no budget for raises this year. I've been here 3 years, I'm sure I'm paid below market, and I'm in the final round with another company. I like my team and would rather stay. We have a 1:1 next Thursday. How should I handle it?

**Response:**

**Diagnosis.** Right now this is set up as positional bargaining: "I want more" vs. "there's no budget." If you push the position, you either lose or end up issuing an ultimatum. Behind "no budget" there are almost certainly interests you can work with.

**Interests.**

| | Position | Interests behind it | Basic needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **You** | "I want a raise" | Pay that reflects your value and the market; staying with a team you like; growth; not feeling taken for granted after 3 years | Economic well-being, recognition |
| **Your manager** | "There's no budget this year" | Keeping a reliable person; staying within the budget they were given; not setting a precedent for the whole team; looking competent to their own boss | Security (their own standing), control over their budget |
| **Their boss / HR** (the real decision-makers on money) | Not in the room | Predictable payroll costs; fair pay bands; low turnover in key roles | Control, avoiding risk |

*"Why not?" from your manager's chair.* If they say "yes" today: they have to go upstairs and ask for an exception, others on the team may ask too, and it looks like they lost control of the budget. If they say "no": they risk losing you, but that risk feels distant and uncertain. So right now "no" looks safer to them. Your job is to make "yes" easier and "no" more concrete.

- *Shared:* you staying, and staying motivated; a team that isn't disrupted by turnover.
- *Simply different (can be dovetailed):* you care about the total over the next year, they care about this year's budget line. That's why timing-based options (next cycle, a bonus now, a raise later) can work.

**BATNA.**
- *Yours:* the outside offer, but only once it actually exists. Until it's on paper it's a hope, not a BATNA. Find out the real number and terms.
- *Theirs:* replacing you, which means hiring time, onboarding and a market-rate salary for the new person anyway. It's probably weaker than they think.

**Options** (bring at least two, framed as ideas, not demands):
1. A raise from the next budget cycle, with the date and amount fixed in writing now.
2. A role or title change that moves you into a different pay band.
3. A one-off retention bonus now, with base pay revisited in six months.
4. Non-cash value that matters to you: training budget, remote days, a project you want.

**Criteria.** Market salary data for your role and city (2–3 sources), the company's own pay bands, what new hires at your level are getting.

**Plan and phrases.**
- Opening: "I'd like to stay and grow here. I've looked at market data for my role, and I want to work out together how to close the gap. Can we look at it?"
- To "there's no budget": "I understand the budget for this year is fixed. What would need to happen for this to be possible? Who decides on the next cycle, and how can I help you make the case?"
- About the offer: mention it only if it's real and better, as information, not a threat: "To be transparent, I'm in a process elsewhere. I'd rather stay, which is why I'm raising this with you first."

**Trap to avoid.** Threatening to leave when you don't yet have an offer. If they call it, you lose both credibility and leverage.

**Lesson from the book.** This is *interests, not positions* plus *make their decision easy*: give your manager arguments they can take to their boss, the way the book advises helping the other side "get new instructions."

**Your move.** How would you phrase your opening line in your own words? And do you know the market range for your role yet?
