# Principles in detail

Detailed techniques and book examples for people, options and criteria. Read this for a full analysis (mode A), negotiation prep (mode B), a debrief (mode E), or when you need a book example to cite. Principle 2 (interests) is in SKILL.md because it's the core of every analysis.

## Principle 1: Separate the people from the problem (book ch. 2)

Every negotiator has two kinds of interests: in the substance and in the relationship. The two get entangled: "the kitchen is a mess" or "our account is almost empty" is heard as personal blame, and in positional bargaining a hard position reads as "you don't value our relationship." Deal with people problems directly, with people tools — **don't trade substantive concessions for a better relationship**. And **proceed independent of trust**: the method doesn't require trusting the other side.

People problems fall into three categories: perception, emotion, communication. Check each one for them **and for yourself**.

### Perception
- **Put yourself in their shoes.** List your arguments and, next to them, how they see the same facts. Feel the emotional weight of their view rather than studying "a beetle under a microscope." *Book example:* worker Jones thought his foreman was persecuting him by moving him to relief jobs five times in two weeks; the foreman was picking his most reliable man and thought Jones would be flattered.
- **Don't deduce their intentions from your fears.** "Is this what they're doing, or what I'm afraid of?"
- **Don't blame them for your problem; describe it:** facts → need → invitation to solve it together. "The generator has broken down three times this month; the first time we lost a week. We need reliable operation. Let's figure it out together: change contractor, contact the manufacturer, something else?"
- **Discuss each other's perceptions openly.** Take seriously an issue that seems minor to you but matters to them. *Law of the Sea conference:* developed countries postponed the "secondary" issue of technology transfer and stalled the whole agreement.
- **Act inconsistently with their perceptions.** Send a message different from the one they expect. *Sadat flew to Jerusalem* and behaved as a partner, not an enemy.
- **Involve them early: "the process is the product."** Don't arrive with a finished solution; ask for advice, give them authorship. *Example:* a lawyer brought amendments to an insurance commissioner who had written the regulation himself, and was shown the door — the commissioner heard "you made a mistake."
- **Help them save face.** Frame the agreement as following from their principles, past statements and precedents — like a judge citing the law rather than declaring "you lost." *A mayor* accepted the terms once they were reframed as a continuation of his own campaign promises.

### Emotion
- **Recognize and understand emotions.** Write down what you feel (fear, anxiety, anger) next to what you'd like to feel (confidence, calm). Do the same for them, and ask where it comes from: past grievances, threats to status or identity, pressure from their bosses.
- **Make emotions explicit and acknowledge them as legitimate.** "We feel this has been unfair — perhaps unreasonably, but that's how we feel. Do you feel something similar?"
- **Let them let off steam.** Don't interrupt or argue; listen to the end: "Go on — what else?" An angry speech is often aimed at their own constituents.
- **Don't react to emotional outbursts.** A labor-management committee in the steel industry used the rule "only one person can lose their temper at a time."
- **Symbolic gestures:** sympathy, an apology (even if you're not personally at fault — it costs little and pays off a lot), a shared meal.

### Communication
- **Listen actively and acknowledge what is said.** Until they feel understood, they'll keep repeating their case instead of hearing yours. Open with "Let me see if I understand you correctly. You think…" and restate their view so they say "yes, exactly." Ideally state it more persuasively than they did ("You have a strong case…"). Only then give your own view. Understanding ≠ agreeing.
- **Two judges, not a debate.** Negotiation isn't arguing before a third party. Treat the other side as a fellow judge trying to agree with you on one verdict.
- **Speak to them, not to an audience.** Reduce the circle; meet one-on-one, off the record.
- **Speak about yourself, not about them.** Not "you broke your word" but "I feel let down."
- **Speak for a purpose.** Before an important statement: "What do I want to communicate, and why?" Don't reveal prematurely that you're willing to concede.
- **Check how words land in another culture.** *Waldheim* in Tehran in the early 1980s said he'd come as a "mediator" to work out a "compromise." In Persian, "compromise" lacks its positive English meaning and "mediator" means a meddler; within an hour his car was stoned.

### Prevention
Build working relationships before the conflict: personal contact, arriving early and staying late for informal conversation, a small request (Franklin's trick: ask to borrow a book). Appeal to a shared identity: "We're both lawyers / parents / engineers — let's think together about how to meet our interests." Put everyone side by side against the problem: a shared document, a shared whiteboard, a shared goal. *Two shipwrecked sailors* survive only if they turn each other's needs into a shared task.

## Principle 3: Invent options for mutual gain (book ch. 4)

Four obstacles: premature judgment, searching for a single answer, assuming a fixed pie, "solving their problem is their problem."
*The orange:* two children split it in half, though one wanted the fruit to eat and the other the peel to bake a pie.

### Techniques
- **Separate inventing from deciding.** First many ideas with no criticism, then select and improve ("what I like about this idea… it could be better if…"). With the other side, put forward **at least two alternatives at once** so that what follows sounds like possibilities, not an offer.
- **Circle chart:** Problem (what's wrong in the real world) → Analysis (diagnosis, causes) → Approaches (general strategies) → Action ideas (who does what tomorrow). From one good idea, move up to its approach and generate other actions from it.
- **Through the eyes of different experts:** how would a banker, an inventor, a union leader, an economist, a tax expert see it? In a family dispute: a teacher, a doctor, a lawyer, a priest?
- **Weaker but achievable.** If a full agreement isn't possible: procedural instead of substantive (can't agree who pays — agree on an arbitrator), provisional, partial, contingent, "second-order" (at least a list of disagreements).
- **Change the scope:** break the problem into smaller units ("edit the first chapter for a fixed fee") or enlarge it by adding value.
- **Unpack what the demand is for.** *Townsend Oil and Pageville:* the mayor wanted to double the plant's tax. Asking why the town needed the money revealed a shared interest in industrial growth. That opened options such as tax holidays for new plants, a joint campaign to attract industry, and tax cuts for expansion.
- **Shared interests → an explicit shared goal**, concrete and future-oriented ("attract new industry to the town within 3 years"). Then a concession is a step toward the goal, not a defeat. Ask: what will deadlock cost us both?
- **Dovetail differences** ("Vive la différence!"): look for things of little value to you and high value to them. A trade in shares happens precisely because the buyer expects a rise and the seller a fall. Pairs to look for:
  - form ↔ substance, symbolic ↔ practical, prestige ↔ results;
  - economic ↔ political, internal ↔ external, ideology ↔ concrete gains;
  - immediate results ↔ long-term relationship, near ↔ distant future;
  - progress ↔ respect for tradition, precedent ↔ this particular case;
  - time: pay more, but later;
  - forecasts: base pay + performance bonus;
  - risk: low rate while risky, higher once investment is recovered;
  - beliefs: each side is sure it's right → let an arbitrator decide.
- **Ask for preferences:** "Which works better for you, A or B? B? Then how about this version of B…"
- **Make their decision easy:**
  - work on the specific person who decides, not an abstraction ("the insurance company"), and give them arguments for their boss ("help them get new instructions");
  - bring an answer, not a problem; never "propose something and I'll tell you if it's enough";
  - draft the agreement;
  - build on their precedents and past statements;
  - improve the consequences of saying yes instead of threatening: look at your proposal from their side — what would they fear, what would they hope for?
  - if you want an action rather than a promise, leave no padding: it's easier to refrain than to stop midway, and easier to stop than to start something new;
  - **critic test:** how will they be criticized for this deal, and how would they answer?
  - **final test:** can they answer with a single word, "yes"?

## Principle 4: Insist on objective criteria (book ch. 5)

When interests are directly opposed, don't decide by will (who's more stubborn). Decide by a standard independent of either side's will.

- **Types of standards:** market value, precedent, scientific judgment, professional standards, efficiency, costs, what a court would decide, moral standards, tradition, equal treatment, reciprocity.
- **Testing a standard:** independent? legitimate? practical? **Reciprocity test:** would both sides apply it to themselves? ("Do you sign this same contract when you're the buyer?")
- **Fair procedures:** "one cuts, the other chooses" (*Law of the Sea:* the mining company proposes two sites, the international body picks one); agree on terms before roles are known (visitation rights before deciding who gets custody); lot; expert, mediator, arbitrator; final-offer arbitration (as in baseball).
- **Agree on principles first, then on terms.**
- **Three rules:**
  1. Frame it as a joint search: "You want it higher, I want it lower. Let's find a fair price. Which standards can help us?"
  2. Be open to reason about which standard applies. If two standards are equally legitimate, a compromise between them is legitimate. If you can't agree on the standard, ask a third party you both respect only which standard is fairest, not to decide the dispute.
  3. **Never yield to pressure**, only to principle. Threat, bribe, "don't you trust me?", stubbornness — one response: "What's that based on? Let's apply a standard." (Note: the Russian edition mistranslates this rule as «никогда не пытайтесь давить на другую сторону»; in Russian, phrase it as «уступайте доводам, а не давлению» / «не поддавайтесь давлению».)
- **The "matter of principle" trap:** using standards only as ammunition for your position (a police union citing only the cities that pay more) locks you in further. Behave like a judge, not an advocate.
- **Ask for their reasoning:** "What's your offer based on?", "How did you arrive at this price?"
- **Their standard is your lever:** "You cited the sale of the house next door. Let's also look at other sales in the neighborhood."
- **"Company policy," "final offer":** acknowledge the person's constraint, ask what the policy itself is based on, suggest looking into it and meeting again, calmly mention your alternative.
- **When they won't budge:** before walking away, check whether you missed a standard that makes their offer fair. If not, compare the offer with your BATNA and weigh the gain against your reputation as someone who yields only to principle.
- **Several standards?** You don't need to agree on the "one right" standard; standards narrow the gap. Prefer the one the parties used before, the most widely accepted, and the most relevant and current one.
- *House foundation:* you don't haggle over foundation depth; you check building codes for that soil.
- *Tom's insurance claim:* the adjuster offered $6,600, then $7,000 as "company policy." Tom asked how it was calculated and scheduled a new meeting. He set the principle "the amount needed to buy a comparable car" and used the adjuster's own data: a newspaper ad for a similar car priced at $6,800, but with 49,000 miles vs. Tom's 25,000. Then he added, item by item, for low mileage ($450), the radio ($125) and air conditioning. Result: $8,024.
- *Law of the Sea:* an MIT model showed both sides what fee was realistic; positions moved without anyone "conceding."
