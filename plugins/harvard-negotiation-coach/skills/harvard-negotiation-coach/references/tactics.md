# Power, tactics and closing the deal

BATNA, negotiation jujitsu, the one-text procedure, dirty tricks and how to respond, sources of power and closing. Read this when the other side is more powerful, won't play, uses pressure or tricks, and before role-play (mode D).

## When they're more powerful (book ch. 6)

### Bottom line vs. BATNA
- **A bottom line** (RU: «нижняя граница») protects you from the pressures and temptations of the moment, but it stifles creativity, keeps you from learning during the talks, and is often set arbitrarily. It does have one legitimate use: limiting the authority of an agent, or holding a coalition together so the other side can't split it.
- **A BATNA** is your real Plan B: what you will actually do if there's no deal. Without one you tend to be too pessimistic about what happens if talks fail, and too eager to agree.

### Developing a BATNA
1. List everything you could do without a deal.
2. Turn the most promising ideas into real options: get an actual offer, find out the price.
3. Pick **one** best option. Alternatives don't add up: "I'll move to California, write a book, live in Paris" is not one alternative.

- **Trip wire** (RU: «проволочное ограждение»): a threshold somewhat better than your BATNA. An offer below it doesn't mean automatic rejection; it means a mandatory pause to rethink.
- **Your BATNA is your power.** *A town and a factory:* the town wanted the plant's annual payment raised sharply. Its real leverage was a legal alternative: extending the town limits and taxing the plant at the full residential rate, which would have cost the plant even more. With that alternative in hand, the town could negotiate as an equal.
- **Reveal your BATNA** if it's strong and they don't know about it. **Don't reveal it** if it's weaker than they think.
- **Analyze their BATNA** to see what they'll realistically accept. If it's too good, change it legitimately. *Citizens' group vs. an industrial plant:* the plant's BATNA was to ignore them or stall; the group's move was to get the plant's building permit challenged, which made ignoring them costly.
- **Resources ≠ power.** A rich tourist who doesn't know prices is weaker than a poor merchant who will sell the vase to the next buyer.
- **The stronger they are, the more you gain by negotiating on the merits.** Put your knowledge, time, money and contacts into improving your BATNA. Insisting on fair criteria wins on procedure even when you can't win on substance.
- **If both sides have strong alternatives,** the best outcome may be to part ways amicably.
- **Micro-BATNA** (RU: «микроальтернатива»): the best outcome if you don't agree today. "Thank you, let me think it over and come back with a proposal."

## When they won't play (book ch. 7)

### Negotiation jujitsu (they push — don't push back)
- **Don't attack their position; look behind it:** "Why this point specifically? Where does the number come from? Suppose we agree — what happens next?" *Nasser* demanded full Israeli withdrawal. He was asked what would happen to Golda Meir if she announced that on TV tomorrow. He laughed — and agreed to a ceasefire the same day.
- **Don't defend your idea; invite criticism and advice:** "What's the worst part of this option for you?", "What would you do in my place?"
- **Recast an attack on you as an attack on the problem:** let them vent, find the legitimate concern in the accusation, make it shared. "I may have put that badly. What matters to us is…"
- **Ask questions instead of making statements, and pause.** Ask an honest question and stay silent. The other person will fill the silence.

### One-text procedure
RU term in the book: «процедура одностороннего посредничества» (one-text mediation procedure). Use it when bargaining has deadlocked, there are many parties, or one side won't talk: learn each party's interests → write a non-binding draft (distance yourself from it: "I'm not happy with it yet") → ask for criticism, not concessions → revise until the comments run out → recommend the final text → "yes" or "no."
- *An architect and a couple* went through five iterations of a house design this way. *Camp David (1978):* 23 drafts in 13 days, then the US recommended the final text.
- **Who can play the mediator:** a third party; a manager settling a dispute between two subordinates; or, in a many-party negotiation, the participant who most wants any agreement. You don't need anyone's consent to start a draft.

### Reference case: Frank Turnbull and Jones Realty (how the weaker side wins)
A tenant learned he was paying $134/month above the legal rent ceiling ($600 instead of $466). The landlady was hostile. His moves:
1. "Please correct me if I'm wrong…" — facts as a hypothesis, not an accusation.
2. "We appreciate what you've done for us" — supporting the person separately from the problem.
3. "Our only concern is fairness. If the rent is fair, we'll accept it" — an objective principle and willingness to abide by it.
4. To a personal attack ("you just want the money") he replied, "I guess I didn't make myself clear." He conceded a grain of truth and returned to the standard.
5. He deflected "don't you trust me?": trust isn't the issue, principle is.
6. He assumed she had reasons and asked for them: "On what principles did you base the rent?" He raised awkward points as questions, sometimes on behalf of an absent third person ("my friend Paul asked me…"), and restated her position before objecting.
7. He gave both outcomes in one breath: "If we can agree now… if we can't…", describing the alternative (the rent control board) as a possibility he didn't want, not a threat. Its inspector had told the tenants they could stay without paying the excess.
8. He didn't decide on the spot: he had prepared a reason to take a pause.
9. Reasons first, then the proposal. He did not reveal his weak real BATNA: they were moving out anyway and would otherwise have written off the overpaid $670.
10. He let her save face and thanked her. After several meetings she agreed to refund the overpayment; they parted on good terms.

## Dirty tricks (book ch. 8)

**Three kinds:** deliberate deception, psychological warfare, positional pressure. Diagnose which one you're facing.

**How to respond: recognize → name it → negotiate the rules of the game.** A dirty trick is a unilateral proposal about procedure. Don't tolerate it hoping they'll be satisfied — giving in only invites more (*Munich 1938*). Don't retaliate either; that escalates. Conduct principled negotiation **about the rules**, using the same four principles. Name the tactic gently: "Maybe I'm wrong, but this feels like good cop/bad cop. If you need something, let's be direct." Last resort: your BATNA and a calm exit with the door left open.

**Prevention:** agree on the rules at the start. "Before we begin, I'd like to understand how we'll work: are we trying to reach a sensible agreement quickly, or will each of us defend a position until someone gives in?"

| Tactic (RU name in the book) | Response |
|---|---|
| **False facts** («фальшивые факты») | "It's not a matter of trust — I verify everything; that's my practice." Verify matter-of-factly |
| **Ambiguous authority** («размытые полномочия», "second bite of the apple") | Before conceding: "What authority do you have?" If the "final" deal suddenly becomes a draft: "Then it's a joint draft; I'll reconsider too" |
| **Doubtful intentions to comply** («сомнительные намерения») | Build a guarantee into the agreement. "Since you're 100% sure, let's write down the consequence of a breach" (*child support: after two missed payments, the house goes to the wife*) |
| **Question about your limit** | Don't lie and don't reveal: "Let's not tempt each other into misleading." Not disclosing everything is not deception. If you suspect no deal is possible, suggest a trusted third party |
| **Stressful setting** («стрессовая ситуация») | Name the problem without blame: "The sun is in my eyes — let's switch seats or take a break." Meeting on their turf can help: they relax, and it's easier for you to leave |
| **Personal attacks, status put-downs** («личные нападки») | Recognizing the tactic mostly defuses it. Make it explicit, calmly |
| **Good cop / bad cop** | Ask the "good cop" the same principle question: "Why is this price fair?" |
| **Threats** («угрозы») | Ignore it, treat it as unauthorized or said in the heat of the moment, or make it explicit: "I negotiate on the merits; I don't respond to threats." Threats tend to unite moderates and hawks on the other side against you. Use **warnings** («предостережения») yourself: consequences that don't depend on your will |
| **Refusal to negotiate, preconditions** | Explore the interests behind the refusal, offer workarounds (intermediary, written exchange), ask about the principle |
| **Extreme demands** («чрезмерные требования») | "How did you arrive at this number? Which deals is it based on?" |
| **Escalating demands** («эскалация требований», *Malta 1971*) | Point it out, take a break to decide whether to continue, and insist on objective principles |
| **Lock-in tactics** (RU edition: «шантаж», "I promised publicly") | Don't make it the center of the dispute; treat it lightly or as a joke; help them back down without losing face. "My principle is to yield to reason, never to pressure." Be ready to break off |
| **"Hardhearted partner" behind them** («жесткий партнер») | Get the intermediary's agreement on the criterion (in writing), then talk to the "partner" directly |
| **Calculated delay** («сознательные задержки») | Name it. Create an alternative that might disappear. Tie deadlines to objective dates |
| **"Take it or leave it"** («соглашайся или уходи») | Not illegitimate in itself: much fixed-price business works this way, and after long talks you may say it yourself, politely. When it's used as pressure, ignore it and keep discussing options, or discuss it openly: make clear what they lose, then give them a face-saving way out ("That was your last offer before we discussed productivity…") |
| **"Don't you trust me?"** (from the Turnbull case, ch. 7) | A trap: both "yes" and "no" lose. "This isn't about trust; it's about principle" |

**Ethics check for your own tactics:** Would you do this to a close friend? Would you be embarrassed if it were printed in the newspaper? Would a novel cast you as the hero or the villain for doing it? Not disclosing everything is fine; lying is not.

## Sources of power and closing the deal (book: conclusion and "Ten questions")

**Power.** How you prepare and negotiate matters whatever the balance of resources. Sources of negotiating power («переговорная сила») work best combined:
- **a good working relationship:** reliable people influence each other more than tricksters do;
- **communication and listening:** say your doubts aloud so they can resolve them (a buyer who silently rejected a new supplier never let it address his worry about delivery volumes); show you've listened before disagreeing. *The elderly patient* refused a transfer until a young doctor listened and learned he feared yet another separation; with that addressed, the move went smoothly;
- **understanding their interests:** *the radio station:* the minority owner, who was also the manager, demanded an outsized price. The buyer bought only the part of his shares it needed for tax reasons and kept him on as manager, saving almost $1M;
- **an elegant option or procedure:** e.g. a stamp auction where the winner pays the second-highest bid, so everyone bids their true value;
- **legitimacy:** standards as sword and shield;
- **a good BATNA**, communicated so they believe it;
- **commitment:** a clear, firm offer (in writing beats oral; a time-limited offer can help); what follows if they decline, framed as a warning; demands that are clear and doable. **Negative commitment** ("what I won't do") is costly positional behavior; use it sparingly, show you understand their interests, and justify it with objective criteria.

Combine them carefully: threatening to walk away with your BATNA can undermine the legitimacy of your offer and the relationship. Pair it with fairness and attention to their interests. Don't guess who's more powerful: "I'm stronger" makes you complacent; "I'm weaker" paralyzes you.

**When positional bargaining is acceptable:** with strangers when finding out interests would cost more than it's worth. If it drags, shift gears toward interests («смена передачи»). If the other side expects positional bargaining by habit, set realistic time limits and split the talks into phases. The higher the stakes, the precedent, the complexity and the value of the relationship, the more you need the method.

**People problems (Q4):**
- never use relationship threats ("if you really cared, you'd give in");
- concede deliberately when it's worth it (for reciprocity, or because the point doesn't matter), but never to improve the relationship;
- negotiate the relationship explicitly as an issue of its own;
- model the behavior you want instead of "teaching them a lesson".

**Irrational behavior:** first stay calm and rational yourself. Then check your own perception: are they really irrational, did they read your openness as a trick, are their values different, did you fail to communicate? Usually the perception is distorted, not the logic.

**First offer:** don't rush it: explore interests first. You can anchor on a standard without committing: "Let's start from the market rate. In New York it's around $18/hour. Does that work for you?" A seller may open at the highest price that won't scare the buyer off, or the highest an independent expert would call fair. The opening number is not a firm position; clinging to it damages your reputation.

**Path to signing:**
- envision the end from the start: how will they explain the deal to their boss, how will you explain it to yours;
- a framework draft with blanks, marked "tentative, no commitments";
- if a point is stuck, use a fair procedure or an independent expert; average only between legitimate standards;
- firm on interests, flexible on solutions; move only in response to reasons;
- the final offer should flow from the discussion: no surprises, neither "take it or leave it" nor too open-ended;
- a fallback: at least an agreement on what you disagree about;
- be generous at the end: a small final gesture of goodwill, clearly marked as the last;
- leave them feeling it was fair; that makes compliance more likely.

**Not negotiating** makes sense only if your BATNA is strong and the chances of something better are slim. Talking is not conceding. *The bank and the company:* the bank misjudged both BATNAs, its own and the company's, and should also have considered negotiating with the bankruptcy judge.

**Being fair when you could take more:** before squeezing out an unfair advantage, weigh the risk of non-compliance, court, revenge, reputation and regret (*the German tourist and the carpet*).

**Culture and personality:** pace, formality, oral vs. written, directness, time horizon, public vs. private, status of negotiators, firmness of commitments. Take them into account, but without stereotypes, and test your assumptions with questions.

**Trying the method:** start where stakes are low, your BATNA is good, standards are clear and the other side is receptive. Expect a temporary dip while you learn — it's an investment.
